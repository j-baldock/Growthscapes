ibm_is_empty <- function(ibm_long) {
  is.null(ibm_long) || nrow(ibm_long) == 0 || !"date" %in% names(ibm_long)

# Helper: compute per-year biomass accrual by patch (experimental period only).
# Summarises to n_years × 2 rows per scenario before combining.
compute_patch_growth <- function(ibm_long, scen_key) {
  if (ibm_is_empty(ibm_long)) return(tibble())
  ibm_long |>
    filter(date >= exp_start_date, !is.na(ggd)) |>
    mutate(daily_growth_g = weight * ggd, year = year(date)) |>
    group_by(year, patch) |>
    summarise(total_g = sum(daily_growth_g, na.rm = TRUE), .groups = "drop") |>
    mutate(scen_key = scen_key)
}

# ── Seasonal biomass accrual by patch (Cold + Warm and high P scenarios) ──────
compute_patch_growth_seasonal <- function(ibm_long, scen_key) {
  if (ibm_is_empty(ibm_long)) return(tibble())
  ibm_long |>
    filter(date >= exp_start_date, !is.na(ggd)) |>
    mutate(
      daily_growth_g = weight * ggd,
      year   = year(date),
      month  = month(date),
      season = case_when(
        month %in% c(12, 1, 2)  ~ "Winter",
        month %in% c(3, 4, 5)   ~ "Spring",
        month %in% c(6, 7, 8)   ~ "Summer",
        month %in% c(9, 10, 11) ~ "Fall"
      ),
      season = factor(season, levels = c("Winter", "Spring", "Summer", "Fall"))
    ) |>
    group_by(year, season, patch) |>
    summarise(total_g = sum(daily_growth_g, na.rm = TRUE), .groups = "drop") |>
    mutate(scen_key = scen_key)
}

15.3 Relative importance of warm habitat

15.3.1 Annual counterfactual

The patch-level accounting above attributes growth to the patch where it was accrued. It cannot credit effects that warm habitat has on the cold patch (e.g., reduced cold-patch density). Here, the benefit of warm habitat is instead estimated by comparing each Cold + Warm scenario with the Cold only run at the same cold proportion, so that:

\[\text{benefit} = \underbrace{N_{warm}}_{\text{direct}} + \underbrace{(N_{cold} - N_{cold,\,\text{cold only}})}_{\text{indirect}}\]

where \(N\) is net growth (g, signed). Years are paired between scenarios. The Cold only run at 5% cold went extinct and is treated as zero production, so the indirect term at 5% cold is not a true difference. The seasonal decomposition plots show why that is, and how the influence of direct vs. indirect effects vary seasonally. In the Spring and Fall, adding warm habitat has almost entirely direct and positive effects on biomass production by providing fish with important supplemental foraging opportunity during a time when relatively cold temperatures and high densities limit growth in cold-only scenarios. In the summer however, adding warm habitat has indirect, negative effects on the importance of warm habitat as the cold habitat produces less biomass than in the cold-only scenario: summer is increasingly defined by slower growth rates and higher adult mortality (because increased abundances and individual body sizes increase total metabolic demand beyond what the cold patch can support). Direct effects are 0 in the summer because fish do not use the warm patch at this time. The role of warm habitat is mixed in the winter. Indirect effects are generally positive as warm habitat reduces cold patch densities and allows fish to reduce or avoid winter mass loss. When cold habitat proportions are high (95-75%), the direct effects are negative (fish are still losing mass in the warm patch due to strong density dependence), but these negative effects do not outweigh the positive indirect effects. But when cold habitat proportions are moderate-low (50-5%), the direct effects are positive as the warm habitat is large enough to fuel individual growth.

## Relative importance of warm habitat

### Annual counterfactual

The patch-level accounting above attributes growth to the patch where it was accrued. It cannot credit effects that warm habitat has on the cold patch (e.g., reduced cold-patch density). Here, the benefit of warm habitat is instead estimated by comparing each Cold + Warm scenario with the Cold only run at the same cold proportion, so that:

$$\text{benefit} = \underbrace{N_{warm}}_{\text{direct}} + \underbrace{(N_{cold} - N_{cold,\,\text{cold only}})}_{\text{indirect}}$$

where $N$ is net growth (g, signed). Years are paired between scenarios. *The Cold only run at 5% cold went extinct and is treated as zero production, so the indirect term at 5% cold is not a true difference.* Importance is expressed as a fraction of annual Cold + Warm net production, so seasonal fractions sum to the annual fraction.

Here, we can see how the relative importance of warm habitat grows as cold habitat proportion shrinks. Deviations from the 1:1 line indicate conditions under which the relative importance of warm habitat is greater or less than area-proportional expectations.

The seasonal decomposition plots show why that is, and how the influence of direct vs. indirect effects vary seasonally. In the Spring and Fall, adding warm habitat has almost entirely direct and positive effects on biomass production by providing fish with important supplemental foraging opportunity during a time when relatively cold temperatures and high densities limit growth in cold-only scenarios. In the summer however, adding warm habitat has indirect, negative effects on the importance of warm habitat as the cold habitat produces less biomass than in the cold-only scenario: summer is increasingly defined by slower growth rates and higher adult mortality (because increased abundances and individual body sizes increase total metabolic demand beyond what the cold patch can support). Direct effects are 0 in the summer because fish do not use the warm patch at this time. The role of warm habitat is mixed in the winter. Indirect effects are generally positive as warm habitat reduces cold patch densities and allows fish to reduce or avoid winter mass loss. When cold habitat proportions are high (95-75%), the direct effects are negative (fish are still losing mass in the warm patch due to strong density dependence), but these negative effects do not outweigh the positive indirect effects. But when cold habitat proportions are moderate-low (50-5%), the direct effects are positive as the warm habitat is large enough to fuel individual growth.
